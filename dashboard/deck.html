Rush hour on Stevens Creek

5 signals, Mary Avenue to De Anza Boulevard. In our 2024 model with textbook timing, a rush-hour car loses 829 s on average.

These five lights run from Mary Avenue to De Anza Boulevard. We rebuilt the evening rush in a simulator from VTA’s 2024 counts. Under a standard textbook timing plan, the average car loses 829 seconds, about 14 minutes, counting cars stuck waiting to get on the road.

How a traffic light works

A light gives green to one group of moves at a time. That group is a phase. One full round of phases is the cycle, and then it repeats. The green split is how the cycle’s seconds are shared out. Between phases there is a short yellow and all-red gap for safety.

Lights work as a team

Lights in a row share one cycle length. The offset is when each light starts its green on a shared clock. Set the offsets right and a group of cars that leaves on green keeps meeting green. That is coordination, or a green wave. Our tuner sets the cycle, splits and offsets of all five lights at once.

The whole system

Output: a timing plan for an engineer to review.

Left to right: VTA counts and the OpenStreetMap road map become a model of the roads and the cars. SUMO, a traffic simulator, drives every car. The plan search tries a plan, runs it, scores it and tries again, 585 times. We lock the best plan, then test it on 30 rush hours it never saw.

From count sheet to cars

VTA’s count sheets were typed into a table, one row per turn and 15 minutes. Each counted turn is tied to its lanes in the road model. We list every road path through the counted turns, then a least-squares fit picks how many cars take each path so the totals match the counts. Those cars enter the simulator at their start times.

Inside one signal

Each phase gets a minimum green and may stretch to a maximum. Detectors watch for cars: if none arrives within the gap time, the green ends early. That is gap-out. The offset and the shared cycle line the lights up. The tuner may move the settings on the left; the safety timings on the right stay fixed.

How the tuner works

Try 0 was textbook. Fresh rush hours picked the finalist. A separate night search ran 200 tries.

This is Bayesian optimization with Optuna. It is not a neural network. Each try sets 32 numbers for all five lights, runs 5 practice rush hours and scores the seconds an average car loses. Past scores guide the next try. Try 0, the textbook plan, scored 457; try 561, the plan we kept, scored 225.

What it can change, and how we score it

The tuner changes 32 settings and nothing else. The score counts every car that asked to drive. A plan is thrown out if mean side-street, eastbound or westbound results get more than 5% worse than textbook, or if any practice hour has more than 2 extra stuck cars. 201 of the 585 tries broke a rule.

Eastbound got much faster and side streets waited far less. Westbound got slightly slower, 140 to 145 seconds. The average gain does not reach everyone. 5 starting points wait less. 6 wait longer: Stevens Creek, from east of De Anza by 49 s, North Stelling Rd by 32 s, South Stelling Rd by 27 s, and smaller rises for the rest. This plan is not better for everyone.

Why the test is fair

Rush-hour gain confirmed, p ≈ 9e-10. Night side-street delay rose about 3%, and the test did not establish that the increase stayed within 5%.

We committed the score, the limits and the seeds before the search. The tuner practiced only on rush hours 0 to 4, finalists were picked on 5 to 12, and the frozen plan ran once on 73 to 102. Night side-street delay rose about 3%, and the test did not establish that the increase stayed within 5%. Rush-hour and night plans were tested separately; the switch between them was not simulated.

What we would do next

  • Match the model closer to the counts at every signal. Now 48 of 96 counted turn checks land close.
  • Get the city's real signal timing and compare against it, not only a textbook plan.
  • Check every trip group so no street waits longer. Now 6 of 11 groups wait longer.
  • Test on the street with the city, once the model matches the counts more closely.

The method repeats for another road: real counts, a road model, a simulator, a plan search and an unseen test. This plan is tuned for this corridor only.
