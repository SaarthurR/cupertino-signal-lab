How it works

Output: a timing plan an engineer can load into the real lights.

Think of the simulator as a video game of Stevens Creek, and the tuner as a player who keeps changing the light timings and remembers what worked.

Real city counts become simulated cars.

These turn counts generate 17,883 requested trips in SUMO: each car follows a mapped route and obeys the modeled lights.

Source: data/processed/tmc_sources.csv. Counts: data/processed/tmc.csv.

The city reports contain the original field sheets, with a count for each turn every fifteen minutes at De Anza and Stelling. Mary has hourly observations. We turn the counts into scheduled car trips, then SUMO simulates each trip. These are historical counts replayed with different random seeds; they are not recordings of twenty different evenings.

The tuner learns from its own tries.

Try 0 was the textbook plan. Final selection used fresh simulation runs.

This is the loop. The computer sets the five lights, runs 5 whole practice rush hours in the simulator, and gets back one score: the seconds an average car loses. Then the tuner looks at every try so far, builds a picture of which settings scored well, and aims the next try at the promising part, 300 times. Where is the AI? It is not a neural network. It is machine learning search, called Bayesian optimization: it learns from its own tries instead of guessing at random. The textbook plan was try 0 at 396 seconds a car, and the plan we kept scored 270.

What it can change, and how we score it.

The tuner only ever touches these 32 numbers: one cycle length shared by all five lights, when each light starts its green, how long each phase of green runs, the shortest green the main road may get, and how much quiet traffic ends a green early. It is scored on one number, the scored seconds lost per requested car, counting every car that asked to drive, including the ones that never got in. And it may not win by hurting anybody: no direction more than 5% slower than textbook, and no more cars left stuck. That threw out 181 of the 300 tries.

300 plans tried, then a test it could not study for.

The best ten tried 8 fresh runs; try 224 won. Final test: 20 new random seeds, same historical demand.

A tuning tool tried 300 timing plans on 5 practice rush hours. The ten best were then run again on 8 fresh ones, and the winner of that run off is the plan we kept, which is why it is not the very lowest point on this curve. Then, before the final test, we wrote down what would count as a win, so we could not pick a flattering number afterwards.

Our plan cut the time an average car loses by about 23 percent across 20 rush hours it had never seen, and it was better in 18 of the 20. Both directions of Stevens Creek got faster, and so did the side streets, which was the thing we were most worried about breaking.

What we would do next

  • In 1 of the 20 test rush hours our plan left 15 cars stuck where the textbook plan left 0. That broke a rule we set before testing, so finding out why comes first.
  • Only 41 of 96 counted movements matched closely (GEH under 5); better counts come first.
  • Get real counts for Bandley Dr and Saich Way. Our source reports have no counts for those two side streets, so we estimated them.
  • Take it outside the simulator: current counts from VTA, pedestrians and bikes in the model, and a supervised pilot before any real light changes.
